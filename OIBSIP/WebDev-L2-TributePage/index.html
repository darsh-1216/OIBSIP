About Marie Curie

Born Maria Sklodowska on November 7, 1867, in Warsaw, Poland — then under Russian imperial rule — Marie Curie grew up in a household that prized intellectual pursuit despite grinding poverty and political oppression. Her father was a mathematics and physics teacher who quietly nurtured his youngest daughter's curiosity, letting her handle scientific instruments at a time when women were largely excluded from formal education. When the Polish underground "Flying University" offered secret classes to women, the young Maria attended with fierce dedication, absorbing knowledge that the empire sought to deny her. It was an early lesson she would carry for life: that the pursuit of truth demands courage.

In 1891, at age twenty-three, she scraped together enough savings to travel to Paris and enroll at the Sorbonne — one of the very few women to do so. Living in a tiny attic room, often too cold and too hungry to concentrate, she persisted through sheer will. Within two years she had earned a degree in physics — the first woman ever to achieve that distinction at the institution — and followed it swiftly with a second degree in mathematics. It was in Paris that she met Pierre Curie, a brilliant physicist whose open-minded respect for her intellect was as striking as his scientific genius. They married in 1895 and formed one of history's great scientific partnerships: two minds pursuing the same light.

Together the Curies investigated the mysterious rays that Wilhelm Röntgen and Henri Becquerel had recently observed. Marie coined the word radioactivity to describe the phenomenon by which certain elements spontaneously emit energy. Working in a leaky, inadequately heated shed, she processed ton upon ton of pitchblende ore with her bare hands — not yet knowing the radiation she was handling would ultimately claim her life — and in 1898 announced the discovery of two new elements: polonium, named in honor of her homeland, and radium. These discoveries reshaped humanity's understanding of matter and energy at the atomic level, laying essential groundwork for nuclear physics and modern medicine alike.

The death of Pierre in a tragic street accident in 1906 left Marie devastated, yet she channeled grief into resolve. She accepted his professorship at the Sorbonne, becoming the first woman ever to hold a faculty position there. During World War I she designed and personally operated mobile X-ray vehicles — affectionately called petites Curies — that brought life-saving diagnostic imaging to the front lines. She died on July 4, 1934, from aplastic anemia, almost certainly caused by decades of radiation exposure. Her notebooks remain so radioactive that they are stored in lead-lined boxes to this day, a haunting testament to the price she paid and the depth of her dedication.

Life & Achievements

Timeline of a Legend

1867

Born in Warsaw, Poland

Maria Sklodowska enters the world on November 7th in Warsaw, then under Russian imperial occupation. Even as a child, she displays an extraordinary memory and passion for learning that sets her apart from her peers.

1891

Arrives in Paris to Study at the Sorbonne

With money painstakingly saved, Marie travels to Paris and enrolls at the prestigious Sorbonne University — one of a tiny handful of women brave enough to do so — beginning a journey that will transform science forever.

1893

First Woman to Earn a Physics Degree at Sorbonne

Despite poverty and social barriers, Marie graduates first in her class with a physics degree — a historic first for a woman at the institution. She follows this with a mathematics degree just one year later.

1895

Marries Pierre Curie

Marie weds fellow physicist Pierre Curie on July 26th. The marriage unites two extraordinary scientific minds; together they will produce research that changes humanity's understanding of the atom and energy itself.

1898

Discovers Polonium and Radium

Working tirelessly in a makeshift shed, Marie and Pierre announce the discovery of two new elements: polonium (named for her homeland) and radium. Marie coins the term radioactivity, introducing a concept that will anchor a new era of physics.

1903

Nobel Prize in Physics

The Royal Swedish Academy awards the Nobel Prize in Physics jointly to Marie Curie, Pierre Curie, and Henri Becquerel for their research on radiation. Marie becomes the first woman ever to receive a Nobel Prize.

1906

First Female Professor at the Sorbonne

Following Pierre's tragic death in a road accident, Marie assumes his professorship at the Sorbonne, becoming the university's first female professor. She continues her research with unwavering determination, refusing to let grief extinguish her scientific flame.

1911

Nobel Prize in Chemistry

Marie receives a second Nobel Prize — this time in Chemistry — for the discovery of polonium and radium and for her work on the nature of radium. She remains, to this day, the only person to have won Nobel Prizes in two distinct scientific disciplines.

1914–18

"Petites Curies" — Mobile X-Ray Units in WWI

During World War I, Marie develops a fleet of mobile radiography units — quickly dubbed petites Curies — and personally drives them to field hospitals near the front lines. These units perform over one million X-ray examinations, saving countless soldiers' lives.

1934

Passes Away — Legacy Endures

On July 4th, Marie Curie dies from aplastic anemia, a disease directly caused by her lifelong exposure to radiation. She departs knowing her discoveries have laid foundations for nuclear medicine, cancer treatment, and atomic theory — a legacy that continues to shape civilization.

Impact & Influence

An Enduring Legacy

⚛️

Atomic Physics

Marie's research on radioactivity fundamentally altered our understanding of atomic structure. Her meticulous experiments demonstrated that radiation emanated from within atoms themselves — a revolutionary insight that paved the way for quantum mechanics and nuclear physics.

🏥

Medical Advances

The discovery of radium and radioactivity directly enabled radiation therapy for cancer. Today, radiotherapy treats millions of cancer patients annually. The mobile X-ray units she pioneered during WWI became the blueprint for modern diagnostic imaging departments worldwide.

🔬

Scientific Method

Curie's approach to science — patient, rigorous, evidence-driven — set a gold standard for experimental methodology. She believed passionately in reproducibility and transparency, publishing her findings openly at a time when doing so was still considered unusual in many scientific circles.

👩‍🔬

Women in STEM

As the first woman to win a Nobel Prize — and the only person to win two in different sciences — Marie shattered the glass ceiling of academia at a time when women were actively discouraged from intellectual life. She remains the most cited female role model in science education globally.

🌍

International Science

Marie Curie helped found the International Committee on Intellectual Cooperation (precursor to UNESCO) and championed cross-border scientific collaboration. Her Radium Institute in Paris became an international hub that trained researchers from dozens of countries.

🏆

Cultural Icon

Named after her — the curie (Ci) unit of radioactivity, element 96 Curium, the Curie temperature in magnetism, and countless schools, institutes and awards. In 2009 New Scientist readers voted her the most inspirational woman in science history. Her face graced the Polish 20,000-złoty banknote.
